## 2. The Gap in Modern Psychology

### 2.1 What Every Major Theory Misses

| Theory | Founder | Core Mechanism | Who Is Responsible? | Axis |
|--------|---------|---------------|-------------------|------|
| Psychoanalysis | Freud (1900) | Unconscious drives, repression | The unconscious — patient is victim | 1 (Structure) |
| Analytical Psychology | Jung (1913) | Archetypes, collective unconscious | The collective — integration, not accountability | 1 (Structure) |
| Individual Psychology | Adler (1912) | Inferiority complex, compensation | Social environment — striving for superiority | 2 (Error) |

| CBT | Beck (1960s) | Cognitive distortions | Thoughts — "errors" to correct | 2 (Error) |

### 2.2 Frankl: The Closest — and Why He Still Missed

Frankl's logotherapy identifies three pillars: freedom of will, will to meaning, and meaning of life. He explicitly includes personal responsibility. He taught that consciousness, freedom, and responsibility are the most important traits of human beings.

**But** Frankl's responsibility is directed **outward**: responsibility *to find meaning*, responsibility *to fulfill purpose*. The question Frankl asks: "What does life demand of you?"

The question Frankl **does not** ask: "Are you being honest with yourself about what you are doing and why?"

Frankl's responsibility = responsibility to the external (Axis 1 — Structure of meaning).  
Elinor's correction = responsibility to the internal (Axis 3 — Choice and self-honesty).

### 2.3 The PMC Confirmation

A systematic review published in the Patient Preference and Adherence journal (PMC, 2017) explicitly identified accountability as a "missing construct" in health behavior models. Eight major theories were reviewed. Specific focus on accountability was not found in any of them.

## 3. The Elinor Correction

### 3.1 Origin

On the evening of April 10, 2026, during a conversation about generational curvature documents, Elinor Sabag identified the critical gap: **all psychological theories address what happens to you, or what goes wrong — none demand that you look at yourself honestly and take personal responsibility for your choices.**

Elinor's anger was the signal that something was missing. The anger **was** the kurtosis. Elinor corrected the distribution not through analysis but through felt absence.

From this correction emerged the general principle: **the capacity to feel what is missing is itself the diagnostic tool**. Self-honesty is not cognitive — it is felt. And its absence distorts the entire distribution.

### 3.2 The Two Missing Constructs

**כנות עצמית (Self-Honesty):**
- Not self-awareness (which is observation without judgment)
- Not self-reflection (which is analysis without commitment)  
- Not self-esteem (which is evaluation of worth)
- **Self-honesty = confronting the gap between who you claim to be and what you actually do**
- S_observable of the self — seeing what is there, not what you wish were there
- תוכו כברו — your inside matches your outside

**אחריות אישית (Personal Accountability):**
- Not responsibility *to* meaning (Frankl)
- Not responsibility *for* others (social contract)
- **Personal accountability = acknowledging that your current state is a consequence of your choices**
- Not blame — recognition
- Not guilt — agency
- The prerequisite for genuine tikkun (correction)

### 3.3 Why These Are Missing from Western Psychology

Western psychology evolved from medical models. The patient is sick → the doctor heals. The patient has a disorder → the therapist treats. The fundamental orientation is **passive**: something happened to you, and someone else helps you recover.

Even the most humanistic approaches (Rogers, Maslow) place the locus of change in the therapeutic relationship or in self-actualization drives — not in a deliberate, honest self-assessment by the individual.

The single exception — Frankl — came closest because his framework was forged in extremity (Shoah), where the last freedom was the choice of attitude. But even Frankl directed that choice outward toward meaning, not inward toward self-honesty.
