ARC Framework

Domain: Cancer Target Boundedness (Discoveries 124–126, 157)

The Bounded Enzyme Target Hypothesis

Testing Boundedness Scores for Serine–Glycine–One-Carbon and Hypusine Enzymes Against Pathway Topology, Dependency Screens and Tumor Expression, with a Polynomial Folding-Space Formulation

Eliran Sabag | ARC-Public Repository

November 2026

Status: Hypothesis — computational analysis of public data; no wet-lab validation

Abstract

The Sabag Bounded Transformation Principle proposes that systems with bounded local moves have polynomially many observable states. ARC Discoveries 124–126 apply it to pharmacology: they score metabolic enzymes by boundedness (substrates + products + backup pathways) and predict deoxyhypusine synthase (DHPS) as a novel cancer target with maximum boundedness (score 2) and 10.5× predicted selectivity, and SHMT2 as a bottleneck of serine–glycine–one-carbon (SGOC) metabolism. Discovery 157 reimplements the idea as a data pipeline over Open Targets, Reactome and Mondo and produces a 126-disease table. This paper tests those claims against independent public data.

Using Reactome reaction annotations, we find that DHPS scores 4, not 2, under a standard small-molecule cofactor convention, tying it with SHMT1, SHMT2 and PSAT1; a score of 2 is obtained only if spermidine is treated as a cofactor, which also gives 2 to PHGDH, PSPH, DOHH and the MTHFD enzymes. Applying the ARC polynomial formula (|Sobs| = O(nc), λ = √c) with protein length n yields 104.5–1011 observable states versus 32n (≥10178) complete states, but does not separate good from poor targets. What does separate them is absence of a backup: DHPS and DOHH are cut vertices of a linear pathway with no paralog and are essential in 89% and 60% of 1,258 DepMap cell lines, whereas SHMT2 (2%) and PHGDH (1%) are not, consistent with SHMT1 redundancy. TCGA/GTEx expression shows SHMT2, PSAT1, MTHFD2 and TYMS strongly upregulated in lung adenocarcinoma and squamous carcinoma, whereas DHPS is not (median z = −0.25 and +0.14). The data therefore support DHPS as a dependency without a backup, but not as a tumor-selective target, and do not support the 10.5× selectivity prediction.

1. Introduction

Target discovery for cancer is typically framed as search over a very large space of molecules and proteins. The ARC framework argues that the observable space is small: chemical space is ~1060 molecules, yet only ~500 targets are druggable (Discovery 124). Discovery 125 predicts SHMT2 as a cancer target from the SGOC bottleneck, and Discovery 126 predicts DHPS, the enzyme catalysing the first step of eIF5A hypusination, on the grounds that it has one substrate, one product and no backup pathway [R1]. Discovery 157 attempts to generalize the approach as a pipeline that, for each disease, selects an enzyme from public data and scores it.

The biology is well motivated. SGOC metabolism supplies one-carbon units and is upregulated in many tumors [1,2,3,4]. eIF5A is the only protein known to contain hypusine, which is required for translation of polyproline motifs [5,6,7], and the polyamine–hypusine axis has been proposed as a therapeutic target [8,9]. The question addressed here is whether the boundedness score itself predicts which enzymes are good targets, and whether available data agree with the specific predictions.

Research questions Q1. Does the boundedness score, computed from curated reaction data, reproduce the scores claimed in D125/D126?
Q2. Does the ARC polynomial formula for protein folding discriminate among candidate targets?
Q3. Do pathway topology (cut vertices), CRISPR dependency and tumor-versus-normal expression support the predicted targets?

2.2 The D157 table

The D157 pipeline (Python and Rust) maps each of 126 diseases to a Mondo identifier, retrieves associated targets ranked by association score from Open Targets, selects the highest-ranked gene with real metabolic chemistry in Reactome, and records the boundedness score B = substrates + products of its principal reaction. Reaction topology is built from Reactome reactions, and a structural bottleneck flag marks reactions whose removal splits the pathway graph. The complete 126-row table is reproduced in Appendix D. We analysed the delivered table and code without modification except for the analyses below. Two defects were corrected before use: backup pathways were represented as graph vertices rather than added to the score, and the Mondo mapping used the SSSOM table in place of free-text OLS4 search, which returned no results for ICD-10 codes.

2.3 Boundedness score (re-computation)

For 16 genes (14 SGOC: PHGDH, PSAT1, PSPH, SHMT1, SHMT2, MTHFD1, MTHFD2, MTHFD1L, MTHFD2L, ALDH1L1, ALDH1L2, GLDC, TYMS, DHFR; 2 hypusine: DHPS, DOHH) we retrieved every Reactome reaction in which the gene product is a participant, removed non-catalytic entries (gene expression, binding, degradation), and counted non-cofactor inputs and outputs. The cofactor set was ATP/ADP/AMP, GTP/GDP, Pi, PPi, H+, H2O, NAD(P)(H), CO2, O2, CoA, SAM/SAH and GSH/GSSG. The lowest-scoring catalytic reaction defines c.

2.4 Polynomial formula

ARC defines c as the number of elements changed per local move, λ = √c, and the observable space as |S_obs| = O(n^c), against the complete space |S_complete| = 3^(2n) for rotamers (Levinthal formulation) [R2,R3,23]. We set c = B (species changed by the catalytic move) and n = residues, and report log10 nc. As an alternative, c = 2 (crankshaft move) was used for the folding move itself.

2.5 Lattice folding run

We executed the repository's pure gradient-descent folder (protein_folding_pure.rs, c = 1 corner/end moves, serpentine and random-walk starts, 3 restarts) on the real sequences, mapping A,V,I,L,M,F,W,Y,C to H and all other residues to P in the 2-D HP model [24,25,26,27].

2.6 Pathway cut-vertex analysis

Reactions were linked when an output metabolite of one matched an input of another (names normalized for polyglutamate forms). A gene was marked a cut if deleting its reactions increased the number of connected components. The hypusine pathway has three reactions and was analysed directly.

2.7 Dependency and expression

DepMap gene-effect scores (lower = more essential; −0.5 used as dependency threshold) were summarized as median and fraction of cell lines below −0.5. For tumor expression we used TCGA median RSEM and the cBioPortal z-score relative to TCGA normal-tissue samples (mRNA z-scores, reference-normal profile), alongside GTEx lung median TPM. RSEM and TPM were not directly compared.

3. Results

3.1 Coverage and validation of the D157 table

Of 65 monogenic diseases with a known causal gene, the pipeline recovered the gene in 61 (93%), for example PAH (phenylketonuria), GLA (Fabry), GAA (Pompe), HEXA (Tay–Sachs), CFTR, ATP7B and G6PC1. D126 gives DHPS a score of 2 (one substrate, one product). Under the cofactor set in §2.3 the Reactome reaction yields 4, because spermidine and 1,3-diaminopropane are counted. The score is therefore sensitive to the cofactor convention, which the original documents do not specify.

The formula collapses the space by 170–970 orders of magnitude for every protein, reproducing the Levinthal-style reduction of the repository's verification program. It does not discriminate targets: genes with the smallest observable space (PSPH, PHGDH, c = 2) are essential in only 1% of cell lines, while DHPS is essential in 89%. With c fixed at 2 for the folding move, all proteins receive λ = √2 and rank by length alone.

The lattice folding run converged within seconds (<2 s for all 16 sequences, up to 1,020 residues). Energy per hydrophobic residue ranged 0.3–0.45 (DHFR 0.36, DHPS 0.34, SHMT2 0.38, PHGDH 0.36, DOHH 0.45) with no relation to dependency. The number of descent steps was approximately zero because the serpentine starting configuration is already a local minimum under the c = 1 move set, so the run illustrates rapid termination at bounded-move optima but does not test polynomial scaling.

3.4 Pathway topology (Q3a)

In the 20-reaction SGOC graph (initially three components owing to unmatched metabolite names), deleting PSAT1 or PSPH increased components from 3 to 4, consistent with their position in a linear serine-biosynthesis chain; SHMT1 deletion gave 3→5, probably inflated by name matching. Deleting SHMT2, PHGDH (a chain end), MTHFD1/1L/2, GLDC or ALDH1L1/2 did not disconnect the graph. SHMT2 is therefore not a bottleneck in this analysis, consistent with SHMT1 providing a cytosolic route [28]. The hypusine pathway is linear:

eIF5A + spermidine --DHPS--> Dhp-eIF5A --DOHH--> Hyp-eIF5A

so DHPS and DOHH are both cut vertices, and Reactome lists no paralog for either.

4. Discussion

Summary of the hypothesis test (1) The boundedness score as defined is not robust: it depends on the cofactor convention, and with a standard convention DHPS and SHMT2 tie at 4.
(2) The polynomial formula nc reduces any enzyme's space but does not rank targets.
(3) The property that does separate targets is absence of a backup: DHPS and DOHH are linear-pathway cut vertices without paralogs and are broadly essential; SHMT2 has SHMT1.
(4) Tumor-selectivity is not predicted by the score: SHMT2/PSAT1/MTHFD2 are upregulated; DHPS is not.

This is partly in agreement with ARC: D126 identified "zero backup pathways" as the decisive criterion, and D157's correction that redundancy should be graph structure rather than an additive term reflects the same intuition. It is the backup term, not the substrate/product count, that carries the predictive content in these data.

For SGOC, the D125 prediction that SHMT2 is a target is supported by upregulation in lung tumors but not by single-gene dependency; combined inhibition of SHMT1 and SHMT2 is the consistent reading, and the dual inhibitor strategy described in the literature [28] matches that reading. PSAT1 and MTHFD2 are tumor-upregulated, MTHFD2 having been highlighted as a cancer-selective mitochondrial folate enzyme [29], but are not strongly essential in culture.

For hypusine, DHPS inhibition is mechanistically coherent: loss of eIF5A hypusination stalls ribosomes at polyproline motifs [5,6]. The only well-characterized inhibitor, GC7, is a spermidine analogue [30], which does not satisfy drug-likeness criteria such as Lipinski's rule of five [31]. Because DHPS is broadly essential and is not upregulated in tumors, selectivity would have to come from differential sensitivity, dose scheduling, or tumor-specific dependence not captured here. We note that spermidine is the DHPS substrate; foods rich in polyamines would be expected to supply substrate rather than inhibit the enzyme. No data in this analysis address natural-product modulators; a specific hypothesis of this kind is set out in §4.1 for laboratory testing.

4.1 A candidate dietary agent for laboratory testing (Discovery 161)

Following the analysis above, a separate hypothesis was recorded (ARC Discovery 161, November 2026) that a commercial brewer's-yeast supplement may modulate the hypusine axis in lung cancer cells. It is stated here only as a testable proposal; no experimental data exist and no efficacy or safety claim is made.

Tensions with the present data: (i) spermidine, abundant in yeast, is the DHPS substrate; (ii) DHPS is broadly essential and not tumor-upregulated, so selectivity must be measured; (iii) a regulatory mechanism implies the agent need not bind DHPS, and curcumin-like compounds are pleiotropic and frequent assay interferers. The proposed laboratory plan comprises nine steps: fractionation and identification; recombinant DHPS assay with GC7 as positive control [30]; hypusinated eIF5A in A549/H1299 cells; viability and selectivity index against normal bronchial epithelial cells; ROS measurement with N-acetylcysteine rescue; spermidine control; DHPS knockdown and rescue; regulatory readouts (DHPS, DOHH, EIF5A, ODC1, SAT1 expression and intracellular polyamines by LC-MS, with curcumin comparator); and assay-interference and lot-to-lot controls. The hypothesis is rejected if no fraction inhibits DHPS or lowers eIF5A hypusination, if killing is independent of hypusination, if the selectivity index is ≤ 1, if death is not preceded or rescued by ROS changes, if spermidine alone reproduces the effect, or if no regulatory change is seen or the effect is not reproducible across lots. Nothing here supports use by patients, who should rely on their oncology team.

5. Limitations and Open Predictions

  • The cofactor set and the choice of the lowest-scoring catalytic reaction are analyst choices; other conventions change B. The variable c = B identifies species changed per reaction with elements changed per move, which is an interpretation of the ARC formula rather than a derivation.
  • The cut-vertex graph is crude: metabolite name matching fragments the graph, and SHMT1's result is probably an artifact.
  • DepMap measures dependency in culture, not therapeutic index or toxicity; broad dependency may indicate toxicity.
  • TCGA z-scores use TCGA normal samples as reference, not GTEx; RSEM and TPM are not comparable. Only two lung subtypes were analysed, and RNA is not protein activity.
  • The HP lattice folding run uses a 2-D coarse model and a start configuration that is already a local minimum; it does not test polynomial scaling or relate to binding-site chemistry.
  • Reaction-count statistics (B versus drug count) are correlational and affected by selection (genes selected by genetic association).
  • No experimental data are presented. The dietary-agent proposal in §4.1 is a hypothesis for laboratory testing; the source material is uncharacterised (manufacturer, lot, composition) and any claim about it requires the assays listed there.

Open predictions. (P1) Co-inhibition of SHMT1 and SHMT2 will be required for growth inhibition in SHMT2-high lung tumors. (P2) Among SGOC/hypusine enzymes, the absence-of-paralog indicator will predict DepMap essentiality better than B. (P3) DHPS dependency will be uniform across tissues, and therapeutic selectivity will have to be achieved by exposure rather than expression.

6. Domain Statement

Bounded Enzyme Target Statement Counting the species a catalytic step changes bounds the local search space of an enzyme polynomially, but does not by itself identify a therapeutic target. The decisive structural variable is the absence of a redundant route: an enzyme that is a cut vertex of its pathway and lacks a paralog (DHPS, DOHH) is broadly essential, whereas a bounded enzyme with a paralog (SHMT2) is not. Tumor selectivity is an independent axis, measured by expression and dependency, and in lung tumors it favours SGOC enzymes (SHMT2, PSAT1, MTHFD2) over the hypusine axis.
